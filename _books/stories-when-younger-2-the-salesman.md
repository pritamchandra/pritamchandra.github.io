It was very unlikely that we were both sitting on the high edge of the terrace. Actually, we didn't fit anywhere else on that morning. We were both outcasts to our own worlds. We were always among people stuck in inappropriate surroundings.

You could clearly tell that this person was by far the most awkwardly dressed individual around. The scorching heat didn't stop him from dressing in black. And the brim of his hat grazed right above his nose; concealing the pair of his mysterious eyes. I had no clue about where he came from.

So, it was him breaking the silence; because, it never is me.

&ldquo;Why are you here?&rdquo;

I was there because I didn't want to be anywhere else. Wherever you be you would either have to talk, or listen. I never talked a lot. And I was tired of listening.

&ldquo;Allow me to help you&rdquo;, he said again.

It is usually hard to get me to speak. But he did. He sensed the weakness. And he was using it against me. And I had to remember things which I wanted to leave far behind.

&ldquo;I don't know. I broke up last week. My father's health is worsening too. Nothing is working out&rdquo;.

My shoulders collapsed in despair. For a moment I thought it was better to keep things to myself. But I also needed to reach out to someone; without being judged or misunderstood. And maybe a stranger was my best chance at it.

&ldquo;Nothing ever does. That's how it works.&rdquo;

&ldquo;Who are you?&rdquo; I realized that this should have been the first thing to ask and know.

&ldquo;I am an agent, you see? A salesman to be precise&rdquo;, he explained.

&ldquo;So, why are you here?&rdquo;

&ldquo;To sell. Quite obvious, isn't it?&rdquo;

And amidst everything I was going through in that moment, the last thing I needed was a salesman shaking me up.

&ldquo;In case you're wondering, I sell death&rdquo;, he continued revealing his unshaped teeth.

I wasn't shocked, but irritated? Certainly. I responded, seemingly annoyed. &ldquo;I don't have time for jokes right now. So please leave.&rdquo;

&ldquo;No dear, it's business. And death can sure be sold. And it works like every other product. I convince people to buy it.&rdquo; He paused. &ldquo;I convince people to die.&rdquo;

&ldquo;And why would you do that?&rdquo;

&ldquo;Because sometimes it's the only way out, the true solution.&rdquo;

&ldquo;Bullshit&rdquo;. I didn't know what this man was after. And for a moment it appeared as if he was present there to amuse me. So I continued pretending to be curious.

&ldquo;So, free service?&rdquo;

&ldquo;Not exactly&rdquo;, and there were the teeth again. With teeth came surprises. &ldquo;Job satisfaction, you understand?&rdquo;

&ldquo;Yes, it's highly satisfying to convince people to die&rdquo;, I mocked, &ldquo;How good are you by the way? How many have you convinced so far?&rdquo;

&ldquo;Well, I'm well off, I suppose. There hardly is any competition. I've convinced people around here as well.&rdquo;

And then, all of a sudden, it occurred to me &mdash; that today wasn't the first time I was seeing him. Quick flashes ran through my head; the backseat behind Farhan anna, the night when he crashed into a running train; the benches before the library, with Ferman, the day before he hung himself. He was there. With people I knew. And they were all dead.

Now I was immensely shocked. And though silence of that morning had somehow subsumed me, my insides couldn't stay calm anymore. My blood boiled with every drop of sweat that formed on my forehead. It felt like my heart was pounding next to my ear. I was more infuriated than scared. And I didn't know how to deal with this. I couldn't bring myself to settle with just words. So I cried in disbelief.

&ldquo;You killed them!&rdquo;

&ldquo;No. I am not a murderer. I just convince people to die. There is a difference, you see.&rdquo;

&ldquo;They had family&rdquo;. I was in tears.

&ldquo;So do you. So does everyone.&rdquo; He placed an arm around my shoulder. I shrugged to remove it. &ldquo;Calm down. Look around. What do you see?&rdquo;

Few yards across the watchtower the gardens ran along the university highway. And the gardeners wandered under the sun, cropping the branches with their oversized scissors. Not a leaf was going to be left jutting out oddly from the row. They were all etching for perfection.

&ldquo;Why do you think they're doing this?&rdquo;

&ldquo;So that they can earn; so that they can feed their wives, parents and children; to afford education, to pay for their needs.&rdquo; I yelled. &ldquo;You see, not everyone is as cruel as you are.&rdquo;

&ldquo;Food, education, needs! What are needs for?&rdquo;

I was never a violent person, but then I seriously considered pushing this man off the roof. &ldquo;Needs are for life. Your cold heart is not going to comply. People to live, not die.&rdquo;

&ldquo;Living?&rdquo; The man laughed out. &ldquo;That's the biggest joke out there. People are too dumb to get it.&rdquo; He paused. &ldquo;No one lives. People just buy themselves time. Everyone you see here is trying to escape; trying their best to escape immediate death; either for themselves, or for someone else.

&ldquo;Fancy anyone. Doctors. Bankers. Labourers. Priests. Everyone.

&ldquo;What is point in planting stones, watering them, and then expecting them to flower one day. Life is fake.

&ldquo;Because death&rdquo;, he adjusted his hat and clenched his knuckles, &ldquo;is inevitable! No matter what you have or what you earn or what you know, you can't buy forever.&rdquo;

It did and didn't make sense at the same time. I had nothing to say in response. I was just hoping that this man leaves, and never comes back. And meanwhile my heart thumped at an abnormal pace. And I felt void forming in my stomach.

&ldquo;Look, dear&rdquo;, he again put an arm, and this time I let it stay. And as he pointed towards the ground he spoke with a fatherly tenor. &lsquo;There, my child, there is the truth. How can you not see it! You don't have to be stiff. You can stop shaking your legs. This is easy and peaceful. Just let go.

&ldquo;Look down there, it's beautiful.&rdquo;

Warm tears rolled down my cheek. My vision blurred. And I leaned to embrace him. &ldquo;I can't do this to my parents. I can't hurt them.&rdquo;

&ldquo;They are already hurt. Everybody is. Why do you live. Eventually, there would not be a thing. Not a stone, nor a memory. You leave now or later, how does it matter?&rdquo;

Choking in my own tears, I asked &ldquo;Can I see Reya once?&rdquo;

And he smiled. &ldquo;Kid, she's gone. There is nothing left for you here&rdquo;

And as I gasped for breath, I asked for the last time &ldquo;Can I never be happy again?&rdquo;

He lifted his hat to look deep into my eyes. &ldquo;I don't know. But what after that?&rdquo; And he wiped my tears with his palm.

He held my hand, &ldquo;Trust me. The pain is about to end.&rdquo; And I looked at the pavement, far beneath the watchtower. And it felt like I could almost reach it with my arm. He whispered. &ldquo;Just let go.&rdquo;

I looked over my head. The sun was blazing brighter than any ordinary day. And I could sense the blue of the sea from the corner of my eyes. Yet the day was getting dark for me. I was seeing things in blur. I was a whirlpool of emotions, but none of it made sense.

I was about to faint. He saw it. And he gave me a reassuring gesture. A gesture to fall.

And I left falling to luck. And as I finally closed my eyes I didn't know which side I'd fall to &mdash; life or death.

Life, it was.

I woke up to heavy raindrops hitting my face. I didn't know how long it had been. My lips had split. Blood and dust had painted a side of my face. And as I stood up to face the sky, the rain washed them away.

The terrace was huge. And as stood against the edge again, I saw people underneath. Sayan, Sid, Poulami. And then, I spotted Reya.

All of them drenched wet; shouting my name at the top of their voices, and scrounging at every corner of the campus. Someone pointed a finger towards the terrace; right where I was standing. And they jumped and waved their hands; they cried my name in unison. And even from the top of the tower I could distinctly make out the spark in their eyes. I felt grateful.

Seeing them I remembered every detail of the morning. They rushed to me in the form of guilt; cold, bare guilt. Guilt of almost being at the edge of life. The rain had washed away my weakness. And as I looked through, I saw clearer than ever. I realised the salesman was gone.

He was never there at all.

I was the salesman.

Not everyone falls towards the side of life. Not everyone is as fortunate. Ferman, Farhan, and many more have fallen prey to the salesman. He is a parasite, wandering around, looking for a host, a prey, a customer of death, at the cost of life. But life, if you do not already know, is non-transactional. Life is priceless.

And such moments which make life priceless are the ones which almost took it from you.

That evening I remember walking out of the door at the base of the watchtower. Reya rushed to give me the tightest hug of my life. And the way she clutched the back of my shirt, I was convinced that she wasn't going to let go very easily. Poulami shouted from behind, in her usual sarcastic tone, &lsquo;The hug is postponed. Calling aunty is prescheduled. There are at least fifty missed calls since morning.&rsquo; Reya turned around. &ldquo;Priorities&rdquo;, Poulami smiled.

But Reya continued to sob on my chest.

And as we strolled back to our hostels, I felt him for one last time; hiding behind the doors; quietly peeking at his failure. I turned back and saw him disappear.

Sid shook my arm and asked who it was. &ldquo;The salesman&rdquo;, I smiled and we left.
