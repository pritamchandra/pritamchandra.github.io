On our way back from the university we pass a small passage between Residence Halls IV and V. The passage is surrounded by a line of trees, and in the evening it gives a generous view of the moon. Tonight, the moon is full and bright. If you are slightly willing and attentive, the moon will always announce itself on full moon days. Over the past few months I have noticed these occurrences, although I am no habitual gazer of the night sky. My first thought was the surprise that a month had already passed since the previous sighting. I must admit that this came only after a brief musing about whether full moon days were a fortnight or a month apart. I was reminded of a dear friend&rsquo;s poem about discarding our time-telling devices and tuning our temporal senses to heavenly objects and their phenomena. My momentary attention to the moon suggested that not only was this a possibility, but also (trivially) desirable.

The last time I had noticed a full moon, I was on a shuttle taking me back to my apartment. This must be a common experience &mdash; motion seems to unlock my creative instincts and my emotional capacity. That night, as the vehicle moved, the moon appeared on the horizon of the dark and wide inner roads of the mostly vacant Education City. It was unusually large, and it lit the entire landscape to the obscurity of silhouettes. At the stretches where the view of the moon was obstructed, it would feel infertile, and unconducive to spirit. In contrast, when the moon would show, it was convivial, evoking a desire for the sight to remain. It would contextualise the movement and provoke an ineffable depth.

On that night, a few friends and I were deciding on a prompt for a short writing exercise. One of the candidates was a version of the following: a man suddenly realises that the vehicle he is in is being teleported to another world. I remember being seated and feeling the premise of that prompt manifest, though devoid of the dramatics. It was not movement as in a hypothetical expectation of a magical passage into another physical realm (although that would be much welcome in my disenchanted, fear-oppressed mind), but movement in the sense of a stirring of the insides. I had a temptation to capture the sight on my camera.

Tonight I am writing with pen and paper, a rarity to say the least. My used, dilapidated notebook appealed to me out of my recently organised space in a manner I could not refuse. Not long before, I was theorising the event of seeing an old friend after a long passage of time &mdash; an event of fundamental joy and attraction, much like the primitive sight of the full moon. Before that, I was casually listening to a lecture on the implications of the overwhelming strides of artificial intelligence. Many of these implications were revealed to me years back in the mould of clinical anxiety. That my early intuitions were not far from those of the current &ldquo;experts&rdquo; was unexpectedly elevating. There is a consensus that doom is impending, and yet, that appears to be no one&rsquo;s functional conviction; not most, at least. A wild faith persists that things will be okay.

What stood out from the AI lecture was the speaker&rsquo;s reference to Christ&rsquo;s call for repentance. I have been living away from the consciousness of my Lord, and because of this I feel estranged from reality. Repentance is critical and, I imagine, also comforting. But I have no clue how to do it.

May God save us from the loss of ourselves.
